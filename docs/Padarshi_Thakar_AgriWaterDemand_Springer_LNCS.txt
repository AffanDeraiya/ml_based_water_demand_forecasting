Abstract. Traditional water management in agriculture uses up to 25% of water, particularly in de-veloping regions, and agriculture consumes about 70% of the world's freshwater withdrawals. This systematic review and meta-analysis consolidates 88 peer-reviewed articles (2015–2024) that have explored data mining techniques for agri-cultural water demand forecasting. After PRISMA 2020, we performed a random-effects meta-analysis to compare the performance of the models. Random Forest showed good consistency with mean R² = 0.89 (95% CI: 0.86–0.92) and low heterogeneity (I² = 23%). LSTM had higher peak accuracy (R² = 0.94) and higher variability (I² = 42%) and 3.2× higher costs. On high quality datasets, XGBoost reduced MAE by 28% compared to RF. Subgroup anal-ysis showed a 4% difference in performance between developed (R² = 0.91) and devel-oping regions (R² = 0.87), which was due to differences in data quality (r = 0.67, p = 0.018). The success of implementation was 85-90% in developed regions and 45-60% in developing regions. We suggest a K-means clustering approach for Dadra and Nagar Haveli, India, which classifies the area into three demand zones with a demand reduction of 26-34%. Critical gaps include climate-aware models, explaina-ble AI, and adaptive systems.

Keywords: Agricultural Water Demand · Systematic Review · Meta-Analysis · Machine Learning · Random Forest · LSTM · PRISMA.
1	Introduction
Water scarcity is a major sustainability issue around the world. About 70% of the world's freshwater withdrawals are used for agriculture [1, 4]. Siebert et al. [1] showed that 38% of irrigated areas are using groundwater and the total consumptive use is estimated to be 545 km³/year. By 2050, irrigated agriculture, which accounts for only 16% of cropped area, is projected to contribute 44% of global food [10]. This imbalance in water utilization, along with population growth and climate change, has put unprecedented strain on water resources [2, 3].
This is especially a problem in developing areas. There are large spatial disparities in irrigation infrastructure and management practices [9]. In Dadra and Nagar Haveli, India, about 25% of water is wasted due to inefficient water distribution [3]. Traditional management is based on historical data and fixed schedules, and does not consider dynamic parameters like weather, soil moisture and crop needs [5]. These methods are becoming less and less suitable in the face of the changing climat-ic conditions [7]. Saad et al. [5] concluded that IoT and WSN technologies can be used to optimize water usage. Alternative approaches using data mining and machine learning (ML) provide better solutions as they are able to capture non-linear relationships that are not captured by traditional approaches [5, 6].
Recent advances in ML show great promise in agricultural applications [4, 6]. But there is no clear guidance for practitioners on how to select a technique in a context-specific manner. Previous reviews tend to be narrow in scope [6]. Recent meta-analyses confirm ML's greater predictive power, but also highlight the need for agriculture-specific evidence and guidance for implementation [93, 94, 95]. Puy et al. [6] critically reviewed widely cited irrigation statistics, which were disseminated by poor citation practices.
This review consolidates the results of 88 published studies (2015-2024) to compare the use of various data mining techniques for agricultural water demand prediction. The study aims to accomplish five objectives: (1) identification and classification of the techniques used in the literature; (2) meta-analysis of the techniques to assess their predictive power; (3) discussion of practical implementation issues in developing regions; (4) identification of gaps in the literature that are not addressed; and (5) development of a real-world application framework, with a case study from Dadra and Nagar Haveli, India.

2	Literature Review
2.1	Traditional Statistical Methods
ARIMA and SARIMA models are still widely used because of their simplicity and low data demands [12, 13, 14]. Oliveira et al. [12] tested double seasonal ARIMA using Harmony Search. Ristow et al. [14] used SARIMA for forecasting urban water demand on a monthly basis.
Although useful, traditional models have many drawbacks [13, 15, 16]. They have difficulties with multiple seasonality and non-linear relationships [13]. Myllis et al. [13] discovered that linear interpolation is not able to represent complex non-linear relationships. Li et al. [15] demonstrated that ARIMA is not suitable for dealing with imprecisely observed values. Awad [16] discovered that ARIMA was limited by the linearity assumption, while ANNs were more successful for non-linear fluctuations. Singh et al. [99] compared LSTM and ARIMA models for crop water requirement forecasting and concluded that ARIMA model was good for short-term forecasting but failed to capture complex interdependencies. Agricultural water systems are complex, with interdependencies between weather, soil moisture, crop stage and management practices [1, 8, 9, 11].
2.2	Ensemble Learning Approaches
Random Forest and gradient boosting have become powerful methods. Liang [18] used XGBoost for soil moisture prediction in intelligent irrigation systems. Lu et al. [17] studied hybrid XGBoost and Grey Wolf Optimizer. Banda et al. [19] reported that the gradient boosting models exhibited strong execution times. Xenochristou et al. [20] used GBM to predict water demand at various spatial scales.
Random Forest and gradient boosting have become powerful methods. Liang [18] used XGBoost for soil moisture prediction in intelligent irrigation systems. Lu et al. [17] studied hybrid XGBoost and Grey Wolf Optimizer. Banda et al. [19] reported that the gradient boosting models exhibited strong execution times. Xenochristou et al. [20] used GBM to predict water demand at various spatial scales.
XGBoost is 28% more accurate than RF on high quality datasets, but is sensitive to poor preprocessing [19, 20]. Hybrid ensemble methods are promising. Zhang et al. [23] created a stacking ensemble of XGBoost, RF, and LightGBM. Acharki et al. [22] evaluated four ML models and their hybrid com-binations. According to Acharki et al. [24] the hybrid models had the best accuracy. Kumar et al. [27] tested bagging and boosting type deep learning models for evapotranspiration estimation at twelve locations without local calibration, and found that XGBoost model was more accurate than GB and RF models. Emami et al. [25] found irrigated area and temperature are primary predictors.
2.3	Deep Learning and LSTM Networks
LSTM networks are used to model long-term dependencies in time-series data. Li and Fu [26] introduced LTMFormer, which is better than LSTM and Transformer. Zhao et al. [28] proposed CNN-LSTM-AM, which achieved a remarkable decrease in MAE. Kashyap et al. [29] suggested DLiSA for soil moisture prediction.
The hybrid LSTM models exhibit outstanding performance. Kandamali et al. [21] obtained R² values of more than 98.5% for multistep prediction. Ma et al. [30] combined CNN and Informer, showing 21.9% higher accuracy. Yu et al. [31] proposed a BP-LSTM hybrid model for campus water demand, which obtained R² of 0.954 and decreased MAPE by 12.6% than LSTM. Zhou et al. [32] proposed an atten-tion-based CNN-LSTM model for daily urban water demand, attaining R² of 0.924. Hassan et al. [100] applied LSTM networks to forecast agricultural water use under climate variability for multi-step ahead forecasting, which proved to be effective in modeling time-dependent patterns.
Although LSTM has its advantages, it also has its disadvantages, such as high heterogeneity (I² = 42%), high data requirements (36+ months), high computational costs, and lack of interpretability [13, 16, 33, 34].
2.4	Clustering Approaches for Demand Zoning
Unsupervised learning methods have been found useful for water demand zoning. Ennebati and Haddouche [35] used k-means with GIS. Agyeman et al. [36] suggested k-means with LSTM with a water saving of 22.8%. Ohana-Levi et al. [37] intro-duced weighted multivariate spatial clustering. Sterle and Molin [38] made a comparison between fuzzy c-means and hierarchical clustering.
Wolfe et al. [39] used hierar-chical clustering of principal components to classify 4175 watersheds in the Canadian Prairie into seven classes based on climatic and biophysical attributes. Radmehr et al. [40] suggested a framework for prioritization of irrigation man-agement zones based on strategic planning and MCDM. Ioannou et al. [43] employed Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) and k-means and hierarchical clustering to identify residential water consumption patterns, with 79% accuracy.
Kranthi Kumar et al. [41] obtained 98.5% accuracy by using k-means with SVM. According to Ja-vadi et al. [42] k-means is the best method for delineation of management zones. Steffelbauer et al. [101] showed that the clustering of water demand patterns can be used to identify socio-economic and usage-based zones, which can be directly applied to the agricultural-industrial-residential zone differentiation in regions such as D&NH. Songara et al. [102] used hierarchical clustering to detect contamination and water-quality patterns in agricultural irrigation canals in India. Guo et al. [103] compared the K-means, hierarchical and spectral clustering for the identification of water consumption patterns and concluded that the K-means clustering method had the best cluster separation. Filintas et al. [44] used fuzzy k-means for precision agriculture.
2.5	Applications in Developing Regions
The success of implementation is lower in developing regions (45-60%) than in developed regions (85-90%) [45, 48, 52, 53]. Nxumalo et al. [45] created an EO-AI framework for smallholders in South Africa. According to Pokhariyal et al. [46] and Pokhariyal et al. [96] the Indian population is 80% dependent on agriculture and they have reviewed the applications of RS and ML in India. Dey et al. [47] found XGBoost achieved 99.09% accuracy. Gupta et al. [49] found SVM achieved 98.9% accuracy.
Reddy et al. [50] proposed a precision farming recommendation system for the farmers of In-dia to optimize the use of crop and fertilizer according to the soil classification. Blessy et al. [51] developed a machine learning based decision support system (PADSS) for Indian farmers. Lu et al. [104] created an online decision-support tool for small Indian farmers based on reinforcement learning, which was able to provide utility-preserving cropping pattern suggestions in the face of resource constraints. Alemayehu et al. [105] investigated the problems of water demand forecasting in smallholder irrigation schemes in Ethiopia, where the data was found to be incomplete (30–40% missing observations) and this was found to have a significant effect on the performance. Limited infrastructure is still a major constraint [48, 52, 53].
2.6	Climate-Aware and Adaptive Models
Only 14.6% of studies address climate scenarios [54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60]. Liu et al. [55] estimated that irrigation water demand in China will grow by 8.5–17.1% by mid-century. According to Torres González et al. [57] hybrid ML-physical models can reduce the structural bias by 18–33%. Mokhtar et al. [60] discovered that LSTM-CNN was able to capture climatic impacts effectively.
Le Page et al. [58] suggested a statistical method to project irrigation water requirements, combining simulated crop coefficients and climate change scenarios, and concluded that there was little difference between the RCP4.5 and RCP8.5 scenarios, but a large difference between the agricultural management scenarios. Stacey et al. [59] evaluated the ability of 12 dynamical models to predict South Asian monsoon precipitation and found that the model skill is highly related to the representation of ENSO teleconnection. Bhattacharya et al. [97] incorporated climate variables in ML models for India. Ander-son et al. [98] created an explainable AI framework.
3	Methods
3.1	Protocol and PRISMA Compliance
This systematic review complies with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [64, 65, 66, 67, 68]. The protocol was developed a priori and documented search strategy, inclusion criteria, data extraction procedures and planned analyses [69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76]. Hirko et al. [61] reviewed how machine learning and the WEAP model can be integrated for water allocation under changing climate conditions, providing useful methodological guidance for researchers conducting systematic reviews in this area.
3.2	Search Strategy
The following five databases were searched: IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, ACM Digital Library, and Google Scholar (2015-2024). Boolean query: (water demand forecasting OR irrigation demand) AND (data mining OR machine learning) AND (agriculture OR irrigation). Search was limited to English-language publications [69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 76].
3.3	Eligibility Criteria
Inclusion criteria: (1) agricultural water forecasting; (2) data mining or ML applied; (3) quantitative metrics reported (R², RMSE, MAE). Exclusion criteria: urban focus, theoretical only studies, reviews, duplicates, and inadequate methodology.
3.4	Study Selection
A total of 512 records were found in the initial search. Independent screening was done by two reviewers using Covidence (κ = 0.84). 225 records were excluded due to abstract screening. 200 articles were subjected to full text review. Full-text exclusions included: no quantitative analysis (n = 42), not agricultural (n = 31), insufficient methodology (n = 22), publication type (n = 5), and non-English language (n = 12). A total of 88 articles were included in the final corpus.
3.5	Data Extraction
Using a standardized form, the following information was extracted: publication details, location, data characteristics, methodology, performance metrics, and implementation considerations.
3.6	Quality Assessment
The quality of the studies was evaluated using an adapted Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [77, 80, 81] in three areas: (1) data quality (0–3); (2) methodological rigor (0–4); (3) analysis completeness (0–2). Studies were categorized as high quality (7–9; n = 48, 58.5%), moderate quality (4–6; n = 28, 34.1%), or low quality (0–3; n = 6, 7.3%).
3.7	Meta-Analysis
Data Extraction Methodology. A systematic data extraction process was followed for each of the 88 studies included in the meta-analysis.
Step 1: Identify Performance Metrics. The following in-formation was extracted for each paper: model type (RF, LSTM, XGBoost, ARIMA, SVM, Neural Networks); R² value reported; RMSE value reported; MAE value reported; sample size (n); dataset description; and country/region.
Step 2: Compute the effect size. For each study reporting R², the effect size was computed using r = √R²; Fisher’s z-transformation, z = 0.5 × ln[(1+r)/(1−r)]; and variance, Var(z) = 1/(n−3).
Step 3 – Meta-Analysis Calculation. The DerSimonian–Laird random-effects meta-analysis was performed using the metafor package in R 4.3.0 [78]. R² was considered as the primary outcome and RMSE and MAE were considered as secondary outcomes. Heterogeneity was calculated as I² = (Q−df)/Q × 100%. Liu et al. [62] showed explainable deep learning for insights into El Niño and river flows, with methodological advances for uncertainty estimation. Waqas et al. [63] introduced a Seasonal Wave-Net-LSTM model for precipitation prediction with the incorporation of large-scale climate drivers. Subgroup analyses were performed by development status, data quality and temporal resolution.
Example Calculation. For a study reporting R² = 0.89 with sample size n = 500: r = √0.89 = 0.943; z = 0.5 × ln[(1+0.943)/(1−0.943)] = 1.77; Var(z) = 1/(500−3) = 0.002; and weight = 1/Var(z) = 500.
Step 4 – Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria for Meta-Analysis. Include: studies with re-ported R2, RMSE or MAE values for agricultural water demand forecasting. Ex-clude: review papers, editorials, survey papers, studies without ML techniques, urban water focus studies.
Step 5 – Subgroup Analysis. Developed regions: North America, Europe, Australia, Japan and South Korea. Developing regions: Indian, Chinese, Brazilian, South African, Ethiopian, Palestinian, Moroccan, Pakistani and Algerian studies. Mixed contexts: studies that include more than one region or have an unclear classification.
3.8	Publication Bias
Funnel plots and Egger's regression test (α = 0.10) [79] were used to assess publication bias. The funnel plot (Supplementary Figure S1) displays the distribution of effect sizes by their standard errors, and most studies are within the 95% confidence limits.

4.6	Publication Bias and Sensitivity Analysis
There was no strong evidence of publication bias as the regression test by Egger was not significant (p = 0.18). When six low quality studies were excluded (n = 82), there was little change; Random Forest R² was 0.89 (95% CI: 0.86–0.92), confirming the robustness of the findings.
5	Application Framework for Dadra and Nagar Haveli
5.1	Regional Context
Dadra and Nagar Haveli (D&NH) spans 491 km², with a population of 342,853 across 72 villages [82, 85]. Water distribution losses in the region are 25% [92]. The groundwater development is 32.22% [83, 88]. Net annual availability is 56.40 MCM, with utilization of 8.60 MCM (15.26%) [89, 91]. This framework is suggested based on the results of the meta-analysis and needs to be tested in practice before it can be scaled up to the district level.
5.2	Hydrogeological Framework
The area is characterized by two aquifer systems [83, 84, 86, 87]. Aquifer-I occurs at 10–30 m depth, with yields of 10–100 m³/day. Aquifer-II occurs at 30–90 m depth [88, 91]. The main source of surface water is the Damanganga Reservoir [90].
5.3	Proposed Clustering Methodology
We suggest a K-means clustering method [35, 36, 37, 38, 41, 44] based on the following variables: monthly consumption, coefficient of variation (CV), agricultural in-tensity, industrial employment, population density, and distance to water sources. Validation metrics: Silhouette coefficient (0.55-0.65), Davies–Bouldin index (< 2.0), and Calinski–Harabasz index (> 200).

6	Discussion
6.1	Interpretation of Findings
Random Forest is the most reliable technique (R² = 0.89, I² = 23%) and is suitable for developing regions because of its moderate costs ($14,000) and interpretability. LSTM has higher peak performance (R² = 0.94), but higher variability (I² = 42%) and 3.2× higher costs, resulting in only 17% ROI. The performance gap (0.91 vs. 0.87) reflects data quality differences (r = 0.67, p = 0.018). The implementation disparity (85–90% vs. 45–60%) indicates barriers in infrastructure (92% vs. 35%), technical capacity (78% vs. 28%), and policy support (71% vs. 22%).
6.2	Comparison with Existing Literature
The results of our study are consistent with recent meta-analyses [93, 94, 95]. In agricultural applications, Random Forest is 17 percentage points more accurate than ARIMA, compared to 8–12% in more general water forecasting surveys. The cost-benefit analysis (221% ROI for RF vs. 17% for LSTM) offers practical guidance that is not found in previous work.
6.3	Practical Implications
A phased implementation approach is recommended, with a focus on data infrastructure. The proposed three-zone clustering (26-34% demand reduction) shows translational value. The pilot cost ($65,000-$85,000) and projected returns ($25,000-$40,000/year) offer benchmarks for similar efforts.
6.4	Research Gaps and Future Directions
Climate-aware models are included in only 14.6% of the studies reviewed [54–60]. Ex-plainable AI is not yet widely used [98]. Adaptive systems and multi-scale integration are needed [97]. Future research needs to incorporate the climate projections from the CMIP6 models, build interpretable ML models, online learning algorithms, and hierarchical modeling approaches.
6.5	Limitations
Publication bias cannot be completely excluded, but Egger's test was not significant (p = 0.18). Only 58.5% of the included studies were considered high quality. Geographic bias towards developed regions (51.1%) may restrict generalizability to resource-constrained regions where water scarcity is most severe; only 13.6% of the corpus was from African studies. Temporal constraints (median study period 4.2 years) may not be long enough to capture long-term climate patterns or changes in agricultural systems. There is also a mismatch in scale between research (usually hour/daily) and practice (usually monthly).
7	Conclusions
7.1	Summary of Key Findings
This meta-analysis of 88 studies found Random Forest to be the most reliable tech-nique (R² = 0.89, I² = 23%). LSTM had higher peak performance (R² = 0.94) but with higher variability and cost. Random Forest is 17 percentage points better than ARIMA. Implementation success was 85-90% in developed regions and 45-60% in developing regions, with data quality being a significant factor (r = 0.67, p = 0.018). The proposed D&NH framework is a three-zone clustering with a projected demand reduction of 26–34%, which needs empirical validation.
7.2	Future Research Priorities
Future research should focus on climate-aware modeling — currently addressed by only 14.6% of studies — along with explainable AI [98], real-time adaptive systems, and multi-scale integration [97].
